## Question: can we measure what is new or missing over time? 

I used to work as a NASA contractor and a small part of my role was trying to ensure
what NASA submitted in it's [data.json](https://data.nasa.gov/data.json) to
[data.gov](https://data.gov/) was accurate and up to date.

[Recent reporting](https://www.404media.co/archivists-work-to-identify-and-save-the-thousands-of-datasets-disappearing-from-data-gov/)
discussed how the total number of datasets on data.gov had decreased over time
based on looking at the total number of datasets reported in [data.gov](https://catalog.data.gov/dataset/).
There was also some discussion about the [Internet Archive Wayback Machine](https://webcf.waybackmachine.org/)
has been useful in preserving old copies of edited or deleted websites, but does not
cover many datasets that require user interactions to download or have other
complications related to size, format, etc.

As (1.) a data.json is required to exist for all U.S. federal government agencies and be available
at an URL, so it can be harvested into data.gov. (2.) And I knew data.json is a json file existing
at the end of an URL so it should be snapshotted by the Internet Archive Wayback Machine, I wondered
if we could use the Internet Archive Wayback Machine to measure changes in agency's data.json over time.

It turns out we can, and I've put some experimental little scripts to do so in a repository
called [data_dot_json_over_time](https://github.com/JustinGOSSES/data_dot_json_over_time).

I have done initial analysis of Department of Commerce, Department of Education, and NASA.
Only NASA shows drops in data in data.json across the January 20th 2025 boundary, but that
may reflect the relevant update speed of some datasets in NASA's data.json versus the other two.
More details found in the repository [data_dot_json_over_time](https://github.com/JustinGOSSES/data_dot_json_over_time).

There's reason this information isn't quite a useful as you might hope. The biggest one being that
data.json's and data.gov lag the actual state of the data. A dataset might be removed, updated, or
unavailable for various reasons for weeks, months, or even in some cases years before it is reflected
correctly in the data.json or data.gov. There's a number of reasons for this. Data.gov is a harvester of
metadata from different agencies and each government agency has it's own process for updating or removing
datasets. Each agency also has many different data systems or platforms that they use to host data and
how and when the state of those systems get reflected in the data.json or data.gov is incredibly variable.
There's very reasonable reasons for this, of course. Different types of data have different storage
and collection requirements. Different types of data have very different usage patterns and so need
access via different types of systems. The end result is the metadata describing datasets elsewhere
you can find in an agency's data.json or in data.gov's catalog is both incredibly useful and highly
imperfect.

_If I continue working on this side project, I will update this blog post with what I learn._

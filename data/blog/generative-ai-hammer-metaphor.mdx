## Introduction

Generative AI refers to machine learning systems that can create new content - text, images, code, audio, or video
based on patterns learned from training data.
In the past few years, these systems have exploded in variety and capability.
[ChatGPT](https://chatgpt.com/) and [Claude](https://claude.ai/) handle conversations and analysis. [Midjourney](https://www.midjourney.com/) and [DALL-E](https://openai.com/index/dall-e-3/) generate images. [GitHub Copilot](https://github.com/features/copilot) writes code.
[Runway](https://runwayml.com/) creates videos. People use them to draft emails, brainstorm ideas, debug software, create marketing materials,
generate art, tutor students, and a thousand other applications we're still discovering.

Mostly in this blog post I'm thinking about text-based generative AI like large language models (LLMs) for writing code
as that is what I use most often, but some of the same ideas apply across modalities.

### Conceptual models and metaphors can help organize thinking

When confronted
with such a transformative and multifaceted technology, our minds naturally reach for metaphors and mental models to
organize our thoughts and make sense of what we're experiencing.

#### What if hammers were new to everyone yesterday?

Imagine, for a moment, that hammers suddenly appeared
for the first time across the entire globe. No one had ever seen one before. Everyone, everywhere, got access to this
simple but powerful tool simultaneously. How would humanity adapt? This thought experiment, absurd as it sounds,
provides a surprisingly useful lens for understanding how society is currently grappling with generative AI.

#### Why write this into a blog post?

While usage of this metaphor is not novel, I've mostly seen it mentioned in passing as a sentence or pargraph.
As it keeps popping into my mind when thinking about generative AI, I wanted to take the time to write
out all the variation of the metaphor that have occurred to me, and explore how they help illuminate
our current moment with generative AI.

## OUTLINE - Sections to Develop

### Low barrier to entry, but skill ceiling is high
- Anyone can pick up a hammer and hit a nail (or their thumb)
- But watch a master carpenter and you see the difference skill makes
- Similarly, anyone can type a prompt, but crafting effective prompts and workflows requires practice
- The gap between "using it" and "using it well" is substantial

### People will hurt themselves and blame the tool
- Early adopters will hit their thumbs, blame hammers, declare them dangerous
- "I tried a hammer once and it was terrible" becomes a take
- Some will call for hammer bans instead of learning proper technique
- Fear-mongering articles about hammer injuries vs. reasoned discussion of safety practices
- Parallel: "AI hallucinations mean it's useless" vs. understanding when and how to verify outputs

### Not every problem is a nail
- When you have a hammer, everything looks like a nail
- People will try to use generative AI for everything, appropriate or not
- Learning when NOT to use the tool is as important as learning when to use it
- Some tasks are better suited for other approaches

### The hammer itself is still evolving
- Imagine learning to use hammers while they're actively changing shape
- Last month's techniques might not work on this month's version
- Ball-peen vs. claw vs. sledgehammer - different tools for different jobs
- GPT-3 vs. GPT-4 vs. Claude vs. specialized models - the landscape keeps shifting

### Different hammers for different jobs
- Not all generative AI is the same
- Code generation tools vs. creative writing vs. image generation
- One-size-fits-all approaches miss the nuances
- Understanding which tool for which task

### Safety and responsible use emerge from experience
- First users will establish safety norms through trial and error
- Best practices will emerge from the community, not just from tool makers
- Some regulation will be necessary, but too early and you build something that doesn't make sense for the world that exists later on.
- Learning when to wear safety goggles (when to fact-check, when to have human oversight)

### Some people will become hammer experts, most will be casual users
- Professional/power users vs. occasional users
- Different expectations and needs from each group
- Danger of assuming everyone should become an expert
- Making tools accessible to casual users vs. powerful for experts

### Economic disruption of new tool availability
- Carpentry becomes more accessible but also professional carpenters face competition
- Some jobs become easier, some become obsolete, new jobs emerge
- The transition period is messy and uneven
- Not everyone benefits equally from new tool access
- Supply chains to build ... hammers or AI infrastructure.

# Writing Plan for "Generative AI Turing Test Parameters" Blog Post

## Overall Arc
The blog post should take readers on a journey from the classic 1950 Turing test to understanding how we're all playing multiple variations of it daily in 2026.
The key insight: **the Turing test isn't about detecting intelligence—it's about detecting when people get fooled**. And by varying the parameters of the original test,
we can map the modified test onto different real-world AI interactions today; thus giving us a system for understanding and navigating them as related
experiences rather than isolated confusing events.

**Key lines**
- ["The Turing test is not a detection of when intelligence appears but when people start being fooled".]

## CASE STUDIES SECTION
**Goal**: Show 4-5 concrete examples where different parameter combinations create different "versions" of the Turing test

### 5. Case Study 1: Facebook bots - The abbreviated Turing test
**Parameters varied**:
- **Time**: Very short (seconds, not minutes)
- **Interaction type**: One-shot or minimal back-and-forth
- **Topic**: Constrained (emotional reactions, short opinions)
- **Bot Goal**: Fooling (appear human enough to not be detected)
- **Evaluator Goal**: Not actively trying to catch the bot (just scrolling)

**What to include**:
- Facebook/social media bots optimize for brevity and vagueness
- They avoid extended conversation that might expose them
- Short, entertaining, emotionally engaging comments
- Often no replies or very generic replies
- **Key quote**: "Facebook bots avoid trying to meet the length and topic constraints of the original Turing test, instead optimizing to be short, entertaining, and vague enough to avoid being caught"

**Connection**: These bots "win" by changing the game - they make the test so short you can't catch them

### 6. Case Study 2: Essay writing in educational settings - The non-interactive test
**Parameters varied**:
- **Interaction type**: One-shot (single artifact produced)
- **Players**: Human + bot collaboration (not just bot alone)
- **Awareness**: Evaluator doesn't know if it's AI-assisted
- **Evaluator Goal**: Evaluating artifact (did student learn?) not catching bot
- **Medium**: Written text (static document)

**What to include**:
- Student submits essay, teacher evaluates
- No back-and-forth conversation
- Twist: Human may have collaborated with AI (not just bot alone)
- Evaluator (teacher) doesn't know if it's AI-assisted or not
- Goal has shifted: not "is this human?" but "did the student learn?"

**Connection**: When the interaction becomes one-directional, the test becomes about artifact evaluation, not conversation

### 7. Case Study 3: Documentation chatbots - When everyone knows it's a bot
**Parameters varied**:
- **Awareness**: Evaluator knows it's a bot from the start
- **Bot Goal**: Being useful (solving user's problem)
- **Evaluator Goal**: Getting help (not catching the bot)
- **Topic**: Constrained domain (company's documentation/policies)
- **Context**: Model + RAG (retrieval from documentation)

**What to include**:
- Customer hitting a help center chatbot
- They KNOW it's a bot from the start
- Success metric: not "is it human?" but "did it solve my problem?"
- Constrained to company's documentation/policies
- Evaluator motivation: frustrated, wants quick answer
- The "Turing test" here is: "Can it be useful enough that I don't demand a human?"

**Connection**: Awareness changes everything - the test becomes about utility, not deception

### 8. Case Study 4: Coding assistants - Speak to it like a human, but remember it's a bot with bot flaws
**Parameters varied**:
- **Time**: Ongoing relationship (days/weeks, not minutes)
- **Interaction type**: Dynamic back-and-forth over multiple sessions
- **Context**: Model + full conversation history + code context
- **Awareness**: Evaluator knows it's a bot
- **Bot Goal**: Being useful in typical coding tasks (responsive to natural language)
- **Evaluator Goal**: Doing task (getting work done efficiently) while maintaining skepticism (not getting fooled)

[Summary of this interaction type that uses the parameters in common language paragraphs based on list above]

**What to include**:
- Many people find they get better results by talking naturally/politely to AI
- But you must simultaneously remember it's not human (to avoid being fooled by hallucinations)
- Give examples of how users can be fooled or mislead by coding assistants...and how remembering how bots can fail helps avoid that
  - Coding assistants can generate plausible but incorrect code
  - They can suggest old or insecure code dependencies as described in https://justingosses.com/blog/instruction-files-to-help-copilot-use-less-risky-dependencies with example of how to use instructions.md files to avoid.
  - They can give plausible explanations that aren't true or accurate
  - They can misunderstand intent, especially as context not supplied
- Explains what the challenge is in this example in terms of being fooled or not fooled. Developers mental model is focused on "task to be completed" and "how might the bot be messing up" is separate thought process.

[Key line: "One of the main challenges of generative AI in this example is the user has to balance instructing the bot as if it was human to get the most out of it but yet still keep in mind all the ways and reasons bots can fail, make mistakes, or mislead.]

**Connection**: Even if you know the bot is a bot, you have to constantly remember it is a bot and has bot's flaws even as you constantly communicate with it as if it was human to get the best results."

- Show original test and its parameters

3. Give concrete example of real-world interaction

- "Turing test = detection of fooling, not intelligence"

By the end, readers should be able to:
- Recognize which "version" of the Turing test they're in
- Adjust their expectations accordingly
- Use this system approach to improve how they think about how they might be being fooled or not by generative AI

### Appendix: Additional Test Design Parameters beyond main ones

#### Evaluator Parameters expanded
- **Evaluator expertise** - Novice vs. domain expert vs. AI researcher
- **Evaluator awareness** - Knows they're being tested vs. unaware
- **Evaluator motivation** - Trying to catch the AI vs. neutral vs. wanting to be fooled
- **Evaluator biases** - Preconceptions about AI capabilities
- **Number of evaluators** - Single judge vs. panel vs. crowd-sourced
- **Evaluator background** - Cultural, educational, professional diversity
- **Evaluator context** - Where they purposely trying to use AI vs. did it show up unexpectedly in an experience where they didn't ask for it?

#### Context Parameters expanded
- **Stakes/consequences** - Entertainment vs. high-stakes decision-making
- **Information available** - No context vs. full conversation history vs. external references
- **Relationship context** - Stranger vs. colleague vs. friend vs. authority figure
- **Cultural context** - Language, norms, shared references
- **Temporal context** - Real-time vs. asynchronous
- **Privacy expectations** - Anonymous vs. identified

#### Measurement Parameters
- **Success criteria** - Percentage fooled vs. degree of confidence vs. specific capabilities
- **Sample size** - Number of test runs
- **Reproducibility** - Consistency across multiple trials
- **What counts as "passing"** - Threshold for success
- **Feedback loop** - Immediate vs. delayed feedback to evaluator
- **Consequences** - What happens if AI passes/fails (e.g., deployment, further testing). Is there a human in the loop to override or fallback or correct?

#### goal 
- **Standard: Catching the bot** - Design the test to maximize chances of detecting AI
- **Inverse: Fooling the human** - Design the test to maximize chances of AI being mistaken for human
- **Not believeing bullshit** - Design the test to maximize/minimize recognition of when you shouldn't trust the AI.
- **Evaluating specific skills** - Focus on creativity, empathy, reasoning, etc.
- **Evaluating ethics/morality** - Can the AI navigate ethical dilemmas as wanted by someone

#### Use Case Scenarios
- **Customer service** - Refer users to correct document or processes, execute small tasks safely? Does it lead more satisfied customers? Does it increase number of enraged customers?
- **Teaching/tutoring** - Is it actually improving learning outcomes?
- **Content generation** - Generates useful writing, images, music, video
- **Creative collaboration** - Brainstorming, writing, design
- **Code review** - Generates successful code with technical accuracy and understanding.
- **Therapy** - Mental health support, coping strategies, etc.
- **Expert consultation** - Semi-professional advice in specific domains
- **Research assistance** - Literature review, data analysis
